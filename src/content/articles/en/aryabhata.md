## A dated work, an incompletely known life

Āryabhaṭa’s *Āryabhaṭīya* is conventionally dated to 499 CE. His statement that he was 23 at the relevant epoch gives the customary birth year 476. His birthplace is uncertain. The work brings mathematical rules together with time reckoning and astronomy; its mathematical section has just 33 verses. The compactness makes later commentary essential to interpretation. [1](#ref-1)

This entry concerns Āryabhaṭa I, not the later astronomer also called Āryabhaṭa. It is tempting to fill the gaps in a famous person’s life with confident stories. A better starting point is the surviving work: what numerical questions does it pose, what instructions does it give, and which details remain dependent on interpretation?

## Reading a circumference rule

One numerical rule yields a circumference of 62,832 for a diameter of 20,000, equivalent to π ≈ 3.1416. The rule gives an approximation; it does not preserve a derivation of that value. [1](#ref-1)

The following calculations are modern illustrations. For a circle of diameter 10 units, using 3.1416 gives a circumference of 31.416 units. Using the modern numerical value of π gives approximately 31.41592654. The difference is about 0.00007346 units. Changing the diameter to 1,000 multiplies that absolute error by 100, even though the relative error remains the same.

This is a useful distinction when assessing historical computation. The absolute error describes a discrepancy in the unit being measured. The relative error divides that discrepancy by the target value. Here the relative error is about 0.000234 percent. A numerical approximation can be impressively useful without being exact, and a very precise answer does not by itself reveal the method that produced it.

We should also distinguish a mathematical relation from a computational recipe. “Circumference equals π times diameter” expresses a general relationship. “Multiply the diameter by 3.1416” is a finite instruction using a chosen approximation. Replacing one by the other silently loses information about accuracy.

## Why notation and commentary matter

The text’s alphabetical number notation packs numerical information into syllables. Clark’s translation discusses this as a means of fitting large numbers into verse, while distinguishing it from evidence about the physical process of calculation. His preface also explicitly describes limitations in the textual material available to him. A translation is therefore a documented interpretation, not a transparent window onto an author’s desk. [2](#ref-2)

For a modern analogy, suppose a notebook records “radius 100; quarter-turn.” Those words can be understood only if we already know the units, the intended operation, and the conventions for angles. Compression saves space but transfers work to the reader or teacher. This helps explain why reading a short rule together with its commentary can be more informative than extracting a formula alone.

## An invitation to calculate and compare

The questions above connect number, geometry, and astronomical practice without requiring us to portray the text as a modern textbook. Try varying a circle’s diameter and calculating both absolute and relative errors. Then ask which error would matter in the particular task being performed. That question makes accuracy a mathematical topic rather than an adjective of praise.

For integer problems associated with this tradition, continue to the [kuṭṭaka](/en/articles/kuttaka/). For another approach to high-accuracy circular measurement, explore [Mādhava’s series](/en/articles/madhava/). Neither comparison establishes a historical route of transmission by similarity alone.
