## Read a problem before a legend

*Līlāvatī* is [Bhāskara II’s](/en/articles/bhaskara/) twelfth-century work on calculation. Its rules and examples range from fractions and proportion to progressions and measurement. The 1817 English translation by Henry Thomas Colebrooke gives readers access to the text alongside explanations attributed to commentators. [1](#ref-1)

A familiar wedding story presents Līlāvatī as the author’s daughter. It is not secure evidence for his family life, nor should it determine how we interpret every address to a learner in the text. [2](#ref-2) The mathematical problems offer a firmer starting point. Ask what quantities are given, which relation is assumed, and how a rule turns that relation into an answer.

## Direct proportion: identifying what stays fixed

The text distinguishes direct and inverse proportion. [1](#ref-1) Here is a modern teaching example, not a translated historical problem. If five identical notebooks cost fifteen units of money, how much do eight cost at the same price per notebook?

The invariant is the unit price: 15 ÷ 5 = 3. Eight therefore cost **8 × 3 = 24**. The traditional-looking three known quantities conceal an assumption: the price per item remains constant. If a seller offers a quantity discount, the proportional model no longer describes the situation.

Checking the units helps explain the operation. Money divided by notebooks gives money per notebook; multiplying by a number of notebooks returns money. This makes the rule intelligible rather than a sequence to memorise. It also reminds us that a valid calculation and a valid model are different achievements.

## Inverse proportion: changing the measure

For a contrasting modern example, imagine thirty-six litres of grain measured into containers. Containers holding three litres require twelve fillings. Containers holding four litres require nine. The product of container size and number of fillings stays fixed:

**3 × 12 = 4 × 9 = 36.**

Increasing one quantity decreases the other. Colebrooke’s Līlāvatī rule 78 similarly concerns measuring the same grain with different measures; our numbers and explanation are newly chosen. [1](#ref-1) A useful question is why inverse proportion applies here. It is the conserved total, not the vocabulary of the story, that justifies the calculation.

## A progression and its explanation

Take another newly constructed example: a reader studies 4 pages on the first day, then 3 more pages each successive day, for 6 days. The counts are **4, 7, 10, 13, 16, 19**. Adding from the ends gives three pairs of 23, so the total is **69 pages**.

In modern symbols the same pairing gives **S = n(a + l)/2**, where a is the first term, l the last, and n the number of terms. For an odd number of terms, the middle term equals half the sum of the two end terms, so the formula still works. Progressions are among the subjects treated in the text. [1](#ref-1)

## Rules, explanations, and later readers

A translation can place a short rule, a worked example, and a commentator’s explanation next to one another. These are distinct layers of evidence. Record which layer supports a claim before attributing its wording or reasoning to the original author. Colebrooke’s notes identify different commentators; they should not be silently merged into a single voice. [1](#ref-1)
